Coordinate systems without the headache

State Plane, grid vs. ground, and the combined scale factor in plain English.

State Plane coordinates take the curved earth and flatten it onto a map. Flattening always stretches or squeezes something. The whole job of a "scale factor" is to tell you how much, so a distance on the map matches a distance you'd tape on the ground.

Grid vs. ground

A grid distance is measured on the flat State Plane map. A ground distance is what you actually measure with a total station or tape at the site's elevation. They're almost never the same. The gap depends on two things:

  1. Where you are in the zone. The projection is only exact along one or two lines. Away from them, the map is slightly too big or too small. That's the grid scale factor, k.
  2. How high you are. The map is built at the ellipsoid, roughly sea level. Up a mountain, the ground is farther from the earth's center, so real distances are a bit longer than at sea level. That's the elevation factor.

The formulas

Elevation factor EF = R / (R + h) Combined factor CF = k × EF Grid distance = Ground distance × CF Ground distance = Grid distance ÷ CF Ground area = Grid area ÷ CF²

R is the earth's mean radius, about 20,906,000 ft. h is the ellipsoid height, which is your orthometric elevation plus the geoid height. In much of the US the geoid height is negative, so h is a bit lower than the elevation on your plans.

A 1,000.00 ft ground distance is 999.85 ft on the grid. That's 0.15 ft per 1,000 ft, about 1 part in 6,800. That's plenty to bust a closure if you mix grid and ground distances in the same traverse.

Where people get burned

Mixing grid and ground

The classic mistake: GPS control in State Plane grid, total station traverse in ground, all in one drawing. Pick one. Then say which on the plat, along with the combined factor you used.

"Ground coordinates" that look like State Plane

Some projects scale State Plane up to ground and keep the big numbers. Now there are two sets of coordinates a few feet apart that look identical. If you do this, scale about a stated base point (not 0,0), change the numbers enough that nobody can mistake them (subtract a round value), and document the factor and base point.

US survey feet vs. international feet

A US survey foot is 1200/3937 m. An international foot is exactly 0.3048 m. They differ by 2 parts per million, which sounds like nothing until it hits a northing of 2,000,000 ft: that's about 4 ft. NIST and NGS retired the US survey foot at the end of 2022, but plenty of older data and state laws still use it. Always check which one a file is in before you combine it with anything.

Grid north isn't true north

Grid north and true (geodetic) north differ by the convergence angle, which grows as you move east or west of the zone's central meridian. A bearing from State Plane coordinates is a grid bearing. If the deed is on true or magnetic bearings, rotate on purpose and say so in the basis of bearings.

What's changing

NGS is replacing NAD 83 with new reference frames and a new State Plane Coordinate System (SPCS2022). Many zones are being redesigned to have much less distortion, and some states are adopting low-distortion projections where grid and ground are within a few parts per million over a whole county. Check the NGS site for the status in your state before you start a long project.

On every plat

Datum and epoch, zone, units (which foot), grid or ground, and the combined factor if you scaled anything. Five lines of text save the next surveyor a week.
