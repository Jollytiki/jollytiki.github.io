Traverse adjustment, step by step

Take a four-sided loop from field angles to adjusted coordinates: balance the angles, carry the bearings around, check the closure, then spread the error with the Compass rule.

This note works one traverse by hand, the same one that loads when you press Example in the Traverse Adjustment tool. Do it on paper once and the tool's numbers will make sense.

The example traverse

Step 1: Balance the angles

The interior angles of any closed figure have to add up to a fixed total:

Sum of interior angles = (n − 2) × 180° = (4 − 2) × 180° = 360°00'00"

Here are the measured interior angles:

The angles are 20" over. Each setup carries about the same pointing and centering error no matter how long the lines are, so the usual fix is to take the same amount off every angle: 20" ÷ 4 = 5". If it doesn't divide evenly, put the odd seconds on the angles with the shortest sights, since those are the least certain.

Before you balance anything, check the misclosure is reasonable. A common rule of thumb allows about K√n seconds, where n is the number of angles and K depends on the instrument and the job (often 10" to 30"). With K = 15", four angles allow 30", so 20" passes. A miss of a few minutes isn't error to be spread around. It's a blunder, so go find it.

Step 3: Latitudes and departures

Latitude = distance × cos(azimuth) Departure = distance × sin(azimuth)

The totals are worked from unrounded values. The departure column only adds to −0.058 as printed. For a loop both totals should be zero. They aren't, and that's the misclosure.

Step 4: Error of closure and precision

The two totals are the legs of a little right triangle. The traverse ends 0.030 ft too far north and 0.059 ft too far west of where it started:

Error of closure = √(0.030² + 0.059²) = 0.066 ft Precision = 1,169.16 ÷ 0.066 = 1:17,807

The tool shows the same numbers, plus the direction of the error: N 63°04'16" W.

Read the direction of the error

A distance blunder pushes the error along that course, so if the error's direction matches one of your bearings (or its reverse), recheck that distance first. A blunder in an angle swings everything after it, and the error runs roughly at right angles to the line from that point to the closing point. Here N 63° W doesn't match any course, which is what plain random error looks like.

Is 1:17,807 good enough? That depends on who's asking. Some states and clients still set a minimum ratio, such as 1:10,000 for boundary work, so check what applies to you. The ALTA/NSPS standards use relative positional precision instead: 0.07 ft (2 cm) plus 50 ppm at 95% confidence, which gives this traverse about 0.13 ft of room. Bigger misclosures than your standard allows mean going back out, not adjusting.

Step 5: The Compass (Bowditch) rule

The Compass rule assumes the error builds up in proportion to distance, so each course gets a share of the correction equal to its share of the total length:

Lat. correction = −(total latitude) × course distance ÷ total length Dep. correction = −(total departure) × course distance ÷ total length

Course 1, for example: −0.0297 × 251.36 ÷ 1,169.16 = −0.0064 in latitude and +0.0585 × 251.36 ÷ 1,169.16 = +0.0126 in departure.

Other ways to adjust

  • Transit rule. Splits the latitude error by each course's share of the total latitudes, and the departure error the same way. The thinking is that angles were measured better than distances. The catch is that the result depends on which way north is, so rotating the same traverse gives a different adjustment. It's mostly seen in old textbooks now.
  • Crandall rule. Holds the balanced angles fixed and puts all the correction into the distances. Useful when the angles are known to be much better than the taping.
  • Least squares. Weights every angle and distance by how well it was measured, adjusts them all at once, and tells you how good each point is. It's the right tool for networks, traverses with extra ties, and anything mixed with GNSS. Most survey software has it built in.

For a simple loop measured with a modern total station, Compass and least squares usually land within a few hundredths of each other.

What closure can't tell you

A loop that starts and ends on the same point can close perfectly and still be wrong. A bad basis of bearings rotates the whole figure, and a wrong EDM scale or prism constant stretches it, without changing the closure at all. To catch those, tie into known control at both ends, or check a line against record. And if you're on State Plane, apply your grid-to-ground factor before you compare.
