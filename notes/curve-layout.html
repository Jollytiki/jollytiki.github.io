Laying out a curve, step by step

Take one horizontal curve from a PI station to stakes in the ground: solve it, station it, build the deflection table, then stake it from the PC or by coordinates.

The Circular Curve Solver gives you the curve's parts, stations and bearings. This note picks up where it stops and works out the layout by hand.

The example curve

A made-up road centerline curve, in feet, staked every 50 ft:

Remember the stationing rule: PC = PI − T, but PT = PC + L, not PI + T. Stations follow the centerline, and on the curve the centerline is the arc.

Step 2: The deflection angle

The angle between the tangent at the PC and the chord to any point on the curve is half the central angle to that point. That's the deflection angle:

δ = l ÷ (2R) radians, or δ° = l × 90 ÷ (πR) l = arc length from the PC to the point

With R = 800, every foot of arc is 2'08.92" of deflection, and a full 50 ft station is 1°47'25.78". At the PT, l = L, so the deflection is always exactly Δ/2. That's your built-in check.

Step 3: The layout table

The first station after the PC is 11+00, which is 28.03 ft along the arc. After that the stations are 50 ft apart, until a short piece at the end to reach the PT. The chord to each point is shorter than the arc:

Two checks before you leave the truck: the last deflection is Δ/2 (12°15'00"), and the last chord from the PC is the long chord from the solver (339.48). If either is off, the table is wrong.

Work deflections from the unrounded arc lengths, and round only the final angle. Adding up rounded 50 ft deflections drifts a second or two by the end of a long curve.

Step 4: Stake it from the PC

  1. Set up on the PC and backsight the PI with the plate at 0°00'00". You're now looking down the back tangent's direction of travel.
  2. For a curve to the right, turn the deflection angle to the right. For a curve to the left, turn it left, or set 360° − δ if you only turn right.
  3. Set each station at its deflection and its distance. With a total station, use the chord from the PC. With a tape, measure the chord from the last station instead and swing it onto the line of sight.
  4. When you reach the PT, check it against the PT you set from the PI along the ahead tangent. They should land on top of each other.
Arc or chord?

Stations are measured along the arc, but you can only measure a straight line between two points. Here a 50 ft arc is a 49.99 ft chord, short by 0.008 ft. That's nothing on this curve, but the difference grows with the cube of the length over the radius squared. On a 100 ft radius cul-de-sac, a 50 ft arc is a 49.48 ft chord, and staking 50.00 puts every point half a foot off.

Moving up on the curve

If buildings, brush or a hill block the view from the PC, move up to the last good station. Backsight the PC with the plate at 0°00'00", plunge the scope, and keep turning the same deflections from the table. It works because the angle between the extended chord and the next chord is always half the arc between them. You don't have to recompute anything.

Step 5: Or stake it by coordinates

With a data collector it's usually easier to give every station a coordinate and stake from whatever control is handy. Start from the PI and walk back along the tangent to the PC, then over to the center:

PC = PI − T along the back tangent (N 60° E) Center = PC + R along the radial (S 30° E for a right curve) Point = Center + R at azimuth (330° + 2δ)

The radial from the center to the PC points back at azimuth 330° (N 30° W). Each point on the curve is that azimuth plus twice its deflection angle, since the central angle is twice the deflection:

Offsets

Edge of pavement, curb and right-of-way stakes go on the same radial lines, at a different radius. For an offset to the outside of the curve, add it to R. For the inside, subtract it. By coordinates that's easy: same center, same azimuth, new radius. By deflections, treat the offset line as its own curve, with its own PC on the PC's radial. The deflection angles stay the same, since the central angles haven't changed, but every chord has to be recomputed with the new radius.

Before you drive the first stake

Check the PC and PT against the plans, and check that the plan's D is an arc definition. Railroads and some older highway plans use the chord definition (a 100 ft chord subtends D), which gives a slightly different radius for the same D. On this curve the chord definition would mean R = 800.52 instead of 800.00. Use the radius on the plans whenever there is one.
