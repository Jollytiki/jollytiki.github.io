Grid to ground, step by step

Take one site from a latitude and longitude to a combined factor, then use it on real distances, bearings, coordinates and areas.

Coordinate systems without the headache explains why grid and ground distances differ. This note is the how-to. It walks one example site through the State Plane & UTM converter and the arithmetic that comes after it.

The example site

A made-up site near downtown Denver, in the Colorado Central zone, worked in US survey feet:

ValueNumber
Latitude39.7392° N
Longitude104.9903° W
Elevation (orthometric, from the plans)5,280 ft
Geoid height (assumed for this example)−55 ft

Look up the real geoid height for your own point with NGS's geoid tools. Don't reuse the one here.

Step 1: Scale factor and convergence

In the converter:

  1. Pick State Plane (NAD83), zone Colorado Central, units US survey feet.
  2. Choose Latitude/longitude → grid.
  3. Enter 39.7392 and -104.9903. If you already have grid coordinates, use Grid → latitude/longitude and enter those instead. You get the same factors either way.
Converter resultValue
Northing1,694,535.460
Easting3,143,339.807
Grid scale factor, k0.99999788
Convergence angle0°19'17.26"
Grid north iseast of true north

The scale factor is almost exactly 1, only 2 parts per million short. That's because Denver sits right on the zone's northern standard parallel, 39°45', where the Lambert projection has no distortion at all. Elsewhere in a zone k is further from 1, but most State Plane zones were designed to stay within 1 part in 10,000.

Which way k changes

In a Lambert zone (wide east to west), k depends only on latitude, so it changes as you go north or south. In a Transverse Mercator zone (tall north to south), k depends on distance from the central meridian, so it changes as you go east or west. The converter shows which projection your zone uses, and you can always move the point and watch k change.

Step 2: Elevation factor

The converter works on the ellipsoid, so it doesn't know your elevation. This part you do by hand. First get the ellipsoid height:

h = elevation + geoid height = 5,280 + (−55) = 5,225 ft

Then the elevation factor, using the earth's mean radius of about 20,906,000 ft:

EF = R / (R + h) = 20,906,000 / (20,906,000 + 5,225) = 0.99975013

Step 3: Combined factor

CF = k × EF = 0.99999788 × 0.99975013 = 0.99974802

That's about 252 parts per million, or 1 part in 3,969. Here the elevation is doing almost all the work: it's more than 100 times bigger than the projection's own distortion. At a mile up, ignoring it would wreck a 1:10,000 survey on its own.

Step 4: Distances

Grid distance = Ground distance × CF Ground distance = Grid distance ÷ CF
  • A total station measures 1,250.00 ft (horizontal, at ground). On the grid that's 1,250.00 × 0.99974802 = 1,249.69 ft. That's 0.32 ft shorter.
  • A GPS-derived grid inverse of 1,000.00 ft is 1,000.25 ft on the ground. Stake it at 1,000.00 and you're a quarter foot short.

The Inverse tool gives you the grid distance between two State Plane points. Divide it by CF for ground.

Step 5: Bearings

A bearing computed from State Plane coordinates is a grid bearing. To turn it into a geodetic (true) bearing:

Geodetic azimuth = Grid azimuth + convergence

Here that's N 45°00'00" E grid → N 45°19'17" E geodetic. That's a 19-minute difference, far too big to ignore if the deed is on true bearings.

Convergence also changes across a site. Two miles east, at the same latitude, the converter gives 0°20'43", almost a minute and a half more. When you rotate to true north, say on the plat where the convergence was computed.

Step 6: Coordinates

To give a project ground coordinates, scale every point about one base point:

Ground N = Base N + (Grid N − Base N) ÷ CF Ground E = Base E + (Grid E − Base E) ÷ CF

A point 2,000 ft north and 1,500 ft east of the base moves another 0.50 ft north and 0.38 ft east. Those shifts grow with distance from the base point, which is why the base point and the factor both have to be on the plat. Then change the numbers enough that nobody can mistake them for State Plane. Subtracting a round number from every northing and easting works.

Step 7: Areas

Ground area = Grid area ÷ CF²

A parcel that's 5.0000 acres from grid coordinates is 5.0000 ÷ 0.99974802² = 5.0025 acres on the ground. That's an extra 110 sq ft, which doesn't sound like much until it's in the legal description. The Area by Coordinates tool gives the grid area when you feed it State Plane coordinates.

When one factor isn't enough

A single combined factor works for a compact site. It starts to fall apart when:

  • Elevation varies a lot. Every 500 ft of elevation change shifts the elevation factor by about 24 ppm. On a site that climbs a ridge, compute a factor for each end of a long line and average them, or use a separate factor for each area.
  • The site is long in the direction k changes. That's north–south in a Lambert zone, or east–west in a Transverse Mercator zone. Run the converter at both ends. If k changes by more than a few ppm, use the average for each line.
  • You need better than about 1:50,000. At that level, compute grid-to-ground line by line rather than with one project factor.
On the plat

Zone, datum and epoch, units (which foot), grid or ground. If you scaled, add the combined factor, how you got it (k, elevation and geoid height used), and the base point. If you rotated, give the convergence and the point it was computed at.
